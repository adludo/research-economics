title: "Leisure: key findings"
description: An evidence map for leisure growth, household work, and the value of free time.
---

**Main conclusion:** Leisure has changed unevenly, unpaid work must be counted separately, and extra free time does not automatically improve well-being.

These are summaries of the supplied discussion, not a systematic review or independently replicated estimates. Follow the linked paper entries for methods, populations, source links, and limitations.

| Study | Key finding | Interpretation boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Becker (1965)](/papers/leisure/#becker-1965-the-opportunity-cost-of-time) | Time has an opportunity cost; wages create competing income and substitution effects. | Theory, not a numerical prediction that wages always increase or decrease leisure. |
| [Gronau (1977)](/papers/leisure/#gronau-1977-separate-home-production-from-leisure) | Home production and leisure are distinct uses of non-market time. | Less paid work can mean more housework rather than more leisure. |
| [Aguiar–Hurst (2007)](/papers/leisure/#aguiar-and-hurst-2007-five-decades-of-leisure-trends) | US leisure rose about 6–9 hours/week for men and 4–8 for women, 1965–2003; gains increasingly favoured less-educated adults. | Ranges depend on leisure definition; leisure inequality is not itself a welfare ranking. |
| [Ramey–Francis (2009)](/papers/leisure/#ramey-and-francis-2009-a-century-of-work-and-leisure) | Whole-population leisure rose only about 4–5 hours/week across the twentieth century. | Schooling, unpaid work, demographics, and the longer period change the comparison. |
| [Ramey (2009)](/papers/leisure/#ramey-2009-home-production-over-the-twentieth-century) | Prime-age women's household work fell about 18 hours/week and men's rose about 13, 1900–2005; per-capita household work across everyone rose slightly. | Do not apply prime-age sex-specific changes to the whole population. |
| [Aguiar et al. (2013)](/papers/leisure/#aguiar-hurst-and-karabarbounis-2013-recession-time-use) | About 30% of lost paid-work time went to home production and about 50% to leisure, much of it sleep/TV. | Approximate shares; the discussion's earlier home-production range was 30–40%. Check the paper's definition and tables. |
| [Krueger–Mueller (2012)](/papers/leisure/#krueger-and-mueller-2012-unemployment-and-emotional-well-being) | Unemployed people reported more sadness and less enjoyment of leisure; sadness decreased on re-employment. | Does not establish that leisure causes unhappiness. |
| [Aguiar et al. (2021)](/papers/leisure/#aguiar-bils-charles-and-hurst-2021-leisure-technology) | The model attributes about 2% lower work hours among young men to improved leisure technology. | Model-based attribution for a specified group, not a gaming experiment or economy-wide effect. |

## Why the trend papers differ

Aguiar–Hurst and Ramey–Francis answer different questions. They use different periods, population coverage, and treatment of schooling and household work. Their headline hour estimates should not be averaged or treated as a direct contradiction.

## What the evidence does not establish

- Less market work always means more leisure.
- People with more leisure are necessarily better off.
- All activities labelled leisure provide equal enjoyment.
- Gaming alone caused young men's declining employment.
