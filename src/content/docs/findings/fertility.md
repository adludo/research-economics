**Birth decisions depend on more than national income.** Development, education, survival, parents' opportunities, and the division of care interact. The available evidence does not identify a universal “more leisure means more births” relationship.

The summaries below were checked against the linked author-hosted publication copies. This verifies support in those texts, not replication of their models or proof of causal claims beyond their designs. [Scope and source versions](/papers/fertility/#scope-and-evidence).

## How fertility connects to GDP and productivity

- **Human capital:** Resources and time invested per child can affect future skills; the growth papers explicitly model this channel. “Child quality” in this literature means investment or human capital, not a judgement about a child's worth.
- **Labour supply:** Births and childcare affect employment and working time, while wages, labour demand, and career opportunities influence fertility choices. Either direction creates reverse-causality concerns.
- **Age structure:** A fertility change alters the balance of children, workers, and older adults over time. A temporary demographic dividend is not the same as permanently higher productivity.
- **Measurement:** Total GDP, GDP per person, GDP per hour, and welfare can move differently. A larger population can raise aggregate output without raising average living standards.

These are mechanisms and accounting distinctions, not a combined causal estimate from the selected studies.

## The leisure connection: what is missing?

The strongest direct time links in this collection concern **childcare burden, employment opportunities, and parenting intensity**, rather than discretionary leisure. The review's §8.1 (printed pp. 224–229) discusses ATUS/MTUS evidence and the need to model parental time more explicitly.

The review also describes US evidence in which rising childcare time is largely matched by lower time in other housework and leisure (p. 227 / PDF p. 77). This is a synthesis of the cited time-use studies, not a new causal estimate or a replication here.

A reduction in paid work may fund childcare, unpaid household work, sleep, or recreation. A fertility study must measure where time actually goes before interpreting it as a leisure intervention. Comparing national working hours with birth rates alone cannot distinguish income effects, family policy, norms, partner selection, or reverse causality.

## Questions worth testing

1. Do predictable schedules or lower total time burdens change birth intentions and later births, separately from pay and employment security?
2. Does access to childcare shift mothers' time into employment, leisure, or other care, and does that alter subsequent fertility?
3. Are different birth outcomes due to delayed timing or a change in completed family size?
4. Do changes in skills, employment, or age structure explain any GDP response, rather than an assumed direct productivity effect?
