**Main conclusion:** The discussion's strongest conclusion concerns real-income levels and consumption opportunities: severe trade restrictions can impose substantial costs. Claims about permanently faster GDP growth after liberalisation are more conditional.

| Study | Reported result | Outcome and inference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Irwin (2005)](/papers/trade/#irwin-2005-the-jeffersonian-embargo) | Embargo welfare loss of approximately 5% of 1807 GNP. | Welfare-equivalent cost, not a measured 5% GDP decline. |
| [Bernhofen–Brown (2005)](/papers/trade/#bernhofen-and-brown-2005-japans-opening) | Upper bound of about 8–9% of GDP for Japan's comparative-advantage gains. | Consumption-opportunity valuation, not annual growth. |
| [Frankel–Romer (1999)](/papers/trade/#frankel-and-romer-1999-does-trade-cause-growth) | Positive estimated effect of trade on income using geography as an instrument. | Mainly income levels; geography's non-trade effects threaten identification. |
| [Rodríguez–Rodrik (2001)](/papers/trade/#rodríguez-and-rodrik-2001-a-methodological-critique) | Limited robust evidence in the reviewed studies that lower barriers independently cause faster growth. | Critique of measurement and identification, not evidence that autarky is beneficial. |
| [Wacziarg–Welch (2008)](/papers/trade/#wacziarg-and-welch-2008-liberalisation-and-growth) | Growth around 1.5 percentage points/year higher after liberalisation, on average, over 1950–1998. | Before/after comparison subject to simultaneous reforms and other confounders. |
| [Feyrer (2021)](/papers/trade/#feyrer-2021-the-suez-canal-natural-experiment) | Income elasticity with respect to trade around 0.25. | A local estimate from partial disruption, not a formula for complete autarky. |
| [Arkolakis et al. (2012)](/papers/trade/#arkolakis-costinot-and-rodríguez-clare-2012-model-based-gains) | Domestic expenditure share and trade elasticity map to welfare gains across a class of models. | Assumption-dependent real-income counterfactuals, not observed growth estimates. |

All numerical results are approximate summaries from the supplied discussion; see [evidence status](/guides/sources/).

## Three distinctions to keep explicit

1. **Low trade versus restrictive policy.** Trade/GDP reflects economic size, geography, prices, and demand as well as policy.
2. **GDP growth versus living standards.** Total GDP, real GDP per capita, productivity, consumption, and welfare measure different things. A level gain need not imply permanently faster growth.
3. **Observed performance versus the counterfactual.** A closed economy can grow; the question is how it would have performed under greater openness.

## How to use these findings

Start with the historical opening/closure episodes, then read the cross-country growth estimates alongside their methodological critique. Use natural experiments and structural models for the questions their assumptions support. Do not pool the percentages above: they refer to different outcomes, horizons, and identification strategies.
