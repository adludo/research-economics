---
title: Closed economies, trade, and income
description: Seven studies of autarky, opening, trade policy, and economic performance.
---

Here, **closure** means autarky or substantial restrictions on international goods trade. It does not mean capital controls or the simplifying closed-economy assumption in a macroeconomic model.

:::note[Evidence status]
These summaries reproduce the supplied discussion's interpretation and approximate estimates, with primary-reference links for follow-up. Full-text verification remains outstanding; see [source notes](/guides/sources/).
:::

## Irwin (2005): the Jeffersonian embargo

**Citation:** Douglas A. Irwin. “The Welfare Cost of Autarky: Evidence from the Jeffersonian Trade Embargo, 1807–09.” *Review of International Economics* 13(4), 631–645.

**Paper:** [NBER working-paper version](https://www.nber.org/papers/w11355).

- **Approach and data:** Historical US trade and commodity prices around an embargo that brought the economy close to autarky.
- **Finding:** The discussion reports the published paper's welfare loss as approximately **5% of 1807 GNP**. Domestic substitution reduced, but did not remove, the cost.
- **Interpretation and limits:** A welfare-equivalent loss relative to GNP, **not an observed 5% fall in GDP**. The working-paper version may differ; check the final publication for the quoted estimate.
- **Data guide:** [Historical episode evidence](/data/trade/#historical-prices-customs-and-national-accounts).

## Bernhofen and Brown (2005): Japan's opening

**Citation:** Daniel M. Bernhofen and John C. Brown. “An Empirical Assessment of the Comparative Advantage Gains from Trade: Evidence from Japan.” *American Economic Review* 95(1), 208–225.

**Paper:** [AEA article](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/0002828053828491).

- **Approach and data:** Japan's nineteenth-century autarky prices, later trade flows, and historical GDP.
- **Finding:** An estimated **upper bound of about 8–9% of GDP** for comparative-advantage gains from trade.
- **Interpretation and limits:** A valuation of improved consumption opportunities, not an annual GDP-growth effect. The bound and historical setting matter.
- **Data guide:** [Historical episode evidence](/data/trade/#historical-prices-customs-and-national-accounts).

## Frankel and Romer (1999): does trade cause growth?

**Citation:** Jeffrey A. Frankel and David H. Romer. “Does Trade Cause Growth?” *American Economic Review* 89(3), 379–399.

**Paper:** [AEA article](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.89.3.379).

- **Approach:** Instruments trade using geographical determinants to address the reverse-causality problem that richer countries may trade more.
- **Finding:** A positive estimated effect of trade on income.
- **Interpretation and limits:** The main estimates concern **income levels**, despite the title. Geography may affect income through non-trade channels, challenging the exclusion restriction. Actual trade is not the same as trade policy.
- **Data guide:** [Country income and trade data](/data/trade/); consult the article for the original vintage and variables.

## Rodríguez and Rodrik (2001): a methodological critique

**Citation:** Francisco Rodríguez and Dani Rodrik. “Trade Policy and Economic Growth: A Skeptic's Guide to the Cross-National Evidence.” *NBER Macroeconomics Annual 2000* 15, 261–338 (published 2001).

**Paper:** [NBER working-paper version](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7081).

- **Approach:** Reassesses influential cross-country studies linking openness and growth.
- **Finding:** The studies reviewed offer limited robust evidence that lower tariff and non-tariff barriers independently explain faster growth.
- **Interpretation and limits:** Measurement and identification critiques, not evidence that autarky improves performance. Trade volume, trade policy, and institutions must be distinguished.
- **Data guide:** [Policy measures and reform dates](/data/trade/#trade-policy-wits-and-unctad-trains).

## Wacziarg and Welch (2008): liberalisation and growth

**Citation:** Romain Wacziarg and Karen Horn Welch. “Trade Liberalization and Growth: New Evidence.” *The World Bank Economic Review* 22(2), 187–231.

**Paper:** [DOI / journal record](https://doi.org/10.1093/wber/lhn007).

- **Approach and data:** Country openness classifications and reform dates, comparing performance before and after liberalisation over 1950–1998.
- **Finding:** Average annual growth was approximately **1.5 percentage points higher after liberalisation** than before in liberalising countries.
- **Interpretation and limits:** An influential before/after benchmark, not a universal causal effect. Stabilisation, institutions, and other simultaneous reforms may affect outcomes.
- **Data guide:** [Openness classifications and reform dates](/data/trade/#wacziargwelch-classifications-and-dates).

## Feyrer (2021): the Suez Canal natural experiment

**Citation:** James Feyrer. “Distance, Trade, and Income—The 1967 to 1975 Closing of the Suez Canal as a Natural Experiment.” *Journal of Development Economics* 153, 102708.

**Paper:** [DOI / journal record](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2021.102708).

- **Approach:** Uses shipping-distance changes caused by the canal's closure and reopening to identify trade shocks.
- **Finding:** An income elasticity with respect to trade of roughly **0.25**, corresponding locally to approximately **2.5% higher income for 10% more trade**.
- **Interpretation and limits:** Supports a causal connection under the identification assumptions. This partial disruption cannot be extrapolated mechanically to complete autarky, and the elasticity is not an annual growth rate.
- **Data guide:** [Income and trade measures](/data/trade/); the episode also requires shipping-route distances described in the paper.

## Arkolakis, Costinot, and Rodríguez-Clare (2012): model-based gains

**Citation:** Costas Arkolakis, Arnaud Costinot, and Andrés Rodríguez-Clare. “New Trade Models, Same Old Gains?” *American Economic Review* 102(1), 94–130.

**Paper:** [AEA article](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.102.1.94).

- **Approach:** Derives welfare gains within a broad class of trade models from the domestic expenditure share and trade elasticity.
- **Finding:** Under the stated assumptions, different models map those two statistics to the same welfare gains.
- **Interpretation and limits:** A framework for **real-income counterfactuals**, not observed GDP-growth effects. Domestic expenditure shares, elasticity choices, and model restrictions determine the result.
- **Data guide:** [Trade flows](/data/trade/#merchandise-trade-un-comtrade-and-wits); bilateral trade totals alone do not supply domestic expenditure or a valid elasticity.

**Reading route:** Irwin and Bernhofen–Brown for historical episodes; Wacziarg–Welch alongside Rodríguez–Rodrik for growth evidence and its critique; Feyrer and Arkolakis et al. for identification and counterfactual methods.
