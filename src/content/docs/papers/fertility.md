---
title: "Birth rates, growth, and time allocation"
description: "Six studies and a review connecting fertility with income, human capital, work, and childcare."
---

This collection asks how birth decisions interact with income, economic growth, human capital, and parents' time. **Market work, childcare, and leisure are different activities.** The selected papers do not establish a universal effect of additional leisure on births.

## Scope and evidence

Six research papers and one review, published 2003–2023, provide a focused entry point. Original author-hosted publication copies were read, with supporting pages identified below. All seven are available through Matthias Doepke's research archive; this author-centred selection is not an exhaustive or systematic review of the field.

[Source provenance and SHA-256 hashes](/data/fertility/sources.json) pin the inspected copies. Paper identity and the listed claims were checked against those copies and the author's bibliography. Independent publisher-record checks, corrections/retractions, current data-provider documentation, and empirical replication remain outstanding. See [fertility audit status](/guides/audit-status/#fertility-collection).

**Reading route:** Start with the review for modern high-income patterns; read the growth papers for historical development; then compare the household-bargaining studies and the wartime labour-supply model.

## Doepke et al. (2023): a new era of fertility research

**Citation:** Matthias Doepke, Anne Hannusch, Fabian Kindermann, and Michèle Tertilt. “The economics of fertility: a new era.” *Handbook of the Economics of the Family*, volume 1, chapter 4, 151–254.

**Sources:** [Author-hosted publication copy](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdoepke/mdoepke.github.io/d25df3c1d402d07977615f81f7cbe39c8ba8bc37/research/Doepke_Hannusch_Kindermann_Tertilt_Handbook_23.pdf) · [DOI printed in the copy](https://doi.org/10.1016/bs.hefam.2023.01.003).

- **Approach:** Literature review and descriptive evidence, principally on high-income economies. It is not another independent causal estimate.
- **Key findings:** The historical negative income–fertility relationship does not describe all modern high-income samples. The review documents a reversal in the cross-country association between female labour-force participation and fertility and emphasises compatibility between careers and family life. Childcare policy, fathers' contributions, social norms, and labour-market flexibility feature in the proposed explanations.
- **Primary locators:** Printed pp. 153–154 (PDF pp. 3–4), §3.1–3.2, pp. 169–174 (PDF pp. 19–24), especially Figure 11 on p. 173; conclusion p. 233 (PDF p. 83).
- **Time-use link:** §8.1, pp. 224–229 (PDF pp. 74–79), discusses parental childcare time, ATUS, and MTUS. On p. 225 the authors identify the direct connection between detailed parenting-time patterns and fertility modelling as an area needing further work. On p. 227 (PDF p. 77), its synthesis of US time-use research describes rising childcare time as largely matched by declines in other housework and leisure. This is a descriptive connection drawn by the review, not evidence that leisure hours themselves cause higher fertility.
- **Limits:** Cross-country correlations are not individual causal effects; findings depend on population and period. The chapter does not imply that income and fertility are positively related everywhere or that its reviewed patterns describe every country today.

## Doepke (2004): fertility decline during the transition to growth

**Citation:** Matthias Doepke. “Accounting for Fertility Decline During the Transition to Growth.” *Journal of Economic Growth* 9(3), 347–383.

**Source:** [Author-hosted publication copy](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdoepke/mdoepke.github.io/d25df3c1d402d07977615f81f7cbe39c8ba8bc37/research/Doepke_JEG_04.pdf).

- **Approach:** A calibrated unified-growth model with joint fertility and education choices, compared with stylised transitions in England, Brazil, and Korea.
- **Key findings:** Education and child-labour policies can change the speed of fertility decline in the model. Child-labour restrictions have larger effects than education subsidies alone in the calibrated comparisons, because forgone child earnings are part of education's opportunity cost.
- **Primary locators:** §6, p. 365 (PDF p. 19); discussion of Figure 4 and policy comparison, p. 373 (PDF p. 27); conclusion, p. 374 (PDF p. 28).
- **GDP/productivity link:** Fertility, schooling, income per person, and the distribution of skills evolve jointly in the model. A higher GDP-per-capita path need not be a welfare improvement for every group; p. 373 explicitly discusses conflicting interests.
- **Limits:** These are calibrated mechanisms, not experimentally identified policy effects. The model's human-capital channel is not a measured effect on total factor productivity, and it estimates no leisure-specific fertility effect.

## de la Croix and Doepke (2003): differential fertility and growth

**Citation:** David de la Croix and Matthias Doepke. “Inequality and Growth: Why Differential Fertility Matters.” *American Economic Review* 93(4), 1091–1113.

**Source:** [Author-hosted publication copy](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdoepke/mdoepke.github.io/d25df3c1d402d07977615f81f7cbe39c8ba8bc37/research/delaCroix_Doepke_AER_03.pdf).

- **Approach:** An overlapping-generations model with fertility and education choices, calibrated simulations, and historical cross-country growth regressions.
- **Key findings:** In the model, differences in fertility across income/education groups change the weighting of children's education in the next generation's human capital. The regressions associate a larger fertility differential with lower income-per-capita growth; including differential fertility changes the estimated relationship between inequality and growth.
- **Primary locators:** Mechanism, pp. 1091–1093 (PDF pp. 1–3); Tables 3–4 and discussion, pp. 1107–1108 (PDF pp. 17–18); conclusion p. 1109 (PDF p. 19).
- **Productivity link:** The channel is skill accumulation and the composition of the next generation, not a direct experiment on hours worked or an isolated estimate of technology growth.
- **Limits:** Sample size, timing of fertility measurements, and endogeneity complicate interpretation; see pp. 1106–1108. The results do not establish a universal causal benefit from reducing births or a policy prescription about any income group.

## Doepke (2005): child mortality and fertility

**Citation:** Matthias Doepke. “Child mortality and fertility decline: Does the Barro-Becker model fit the facts?” *Journal of Population Economics* 18(2), 337–366.

**Sources:** [Author-hosted publication copy](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdoepke/mdoepke.github.io/d25df3c1d402d07977615f81f7cbe39c8ba8bc37/research/Doepke_JPopEc_05.pdf) · [DOI printed in the copy](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00148-004-0208-z).

- **Approach:** Compares calibrated deterministic, stochastic, and sequential fertility-choice models, with an education extension, using historical English mortality conditions.
- **Key finding:** In the calibrated historical comparisons, lower child mortality reduces births but increases surviving children. These variants therefore do not explain the observed decline in surviving-family size through mortality decline alone.
- **Primary locators:** Calibration and quantitative comparison, pp. 344–345 (PDF pp. 8–9); education extension and conclusion, pp. 357–360 (PDF pp. 21–24).
- **Growth link:** It tests one explanation for demographic transition and examines human-capital incentives. It does not identify a modern causal GDP effect of infant survival or birth-rate change.
- **Limits:** Do not generalise the births result to all survival probabilities. The conclusion on p. 359 describes a hump-shaped relationship: at sufficiently low survival probabilities, better survival can increase births. The result is not an argument against health improvements.

## Doepke, Hazan, and Maoz (2015): the baby boom and wartime work

**Citation:** Matthias Doepke, Moshe Hazan, and Yishay D. Maoz. “The Baby Boom and World War II: A Macroeconomic Analysis.” *Review of Economic Studies* 82(3), 1031–1073.

**Sources:** [Author-hosted publication copy](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdoepke/mdoepke.github.io/d25df3c1d402d07977615f81f7cbe39c8ba8bc37/research/Doepke_Hazan_Maoz_ReStud_15.pdf) · [DOI printed in the copy](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdv010).

- **Approach:** Historical evidence and a quantitative general-equilibrium model of female labour supply and fertility around the US wartime and post-war period.
- **Key finding:** The model links persistent labour supply among women who worked during the war to increased competition faced by younger women after the war. Younger women's reduced market opportunities encourage earlier family formation and higher fertility in the model; the authors propose this as an explanation for much of the baby boom.
- **Primary locators:** Abstract, p. 1031 (PDF p. 1); introduction, pp. 1033–1034 (PDF pp. 3–4). These pages state the mechanism and model interpretation.
- **Time/GDP link:** Labour demand and cohort competition affect the opportunity cost of childbearing. This is not evidence that a shorter working week or additional discretionary leisure universally increases births.
- **Limits:** A historically specific model-based explanation, not a randomised leisure intervention or a general rule that female employment lowers fertility. Read alongside the modern high-income review above.

## Doepke and Kindermann (2019): bargaining over babies

**Citation:** Matthias Doepke and Fabian Kindermann. “Bargaining over Babies: Theory, Evidence, and Policy Implications.” *American Economic Review* 109(9), 3264–3306.

**Sources:** [Author-hosted publication copy](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdoepke/mdoepke.github.io/d25df3c1d402d07977615f81f7cbe39c8ba8bc37/research/Doepke_Kindermann_AER_2019.pdf) · [DOI printed in the copy](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20160328).

- **Approach:** Generations and Gender Programme data on fertility intentions, later births, and childcare allocation, combined with an estimated household-bargaining model.
- **Key findings:** Births are much more likely when both partners are reported to want a child. The model makes the distribution of childcare costs between parents relevant to fertility, not just the total cost. Targeted reductions in mothers' childcare burden can raise fertility more effectively than untargeted support in the model.
- **Primary locators:** Main mechanism and policy interpretation, pp. 3264–3267 (PDF pp. 1–4); measurement and sample definitions, pp. 3269–3270 (PDF pp. 6–7).
- **Time-use link:** Childcare responsibilities and career costs can affect intentions and subsequent births. Reducing care burden is not equivalent to providing extra leisure: freed time can go into employment or other activities.
- **Limits:** One respondent reports both their own and their partner's intentions; these are not independent interviews of both spouses. Intentions and linked-outcomes samples differ. Policy comparisons are model-based, not experimental estimates of a subsidy or childcare programme.

## Doepke and Tertilt (2018): empowerment and desired fertility

**Citation:** Matthias Doepke and Michèle Tertilt. “Women's Empowerment, the Gender Gap in Desired Fertility, and Fertility Outcomes in Developing Countries.” *AEA Papers and Proceedings* 108, 358–362.

**Sources:** [Author-hosted publication copy](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdoepke/mdoepke.github.io/d25df3c1d402d07977615f81f7cbe39c8ba8bc37/research/Doepke_Tertilt_AEAPP_2018.pdf) · [DOI printed in the copy](https://doi.org/10.1257/pandp.20181085).

- **Approach:** Descriptive cross-country evidence and DHS couple data, including Burkina Faso and Ethiopia, linking empowerment, partners' desired family sizes, and realised fertility.
- **Key finding:** Women and men can differ substantially in desired fertility. The paper argues that household bargaining and changes in women's position matter for understanding fertility transition, rather than treating the family as a single decision-maker.
- **Primary locators:** §I–II, pp. 358–359 (PDF pp. 1–2); Table 1 and §V–VI, pp. 360–361 (PDF pp. 3–4).
- **Development link:** Education, empowerment, fertility, and human-capital investment can interact, but the reported associations do not isolate an empowerment-driven GDP effect.
- **Limits:** Desired fertility is self-reported and is not the same as a realised birth rate. Cross-sectional associations do not by themselves identify the effect of policy or changes in bargaining power.
