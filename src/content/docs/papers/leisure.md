---
title: Leisure and time allocation
description: Eight foundational and empirical studies of leisure, home production, and well-being.
---

Leisure is a use of time, not simply the absence of paid work. These studies distinguish paid employment, household production, schooling, care, and leisure, and ask how their allocation affects welfare.

**Reading route:** Becker → Gronau for theory; Aguiar–Hurst alongside Ramey–Francis for measurement; then the recession, unemployment, and technology papers.

:::note[Evidence status]
Summaries and approximate estimates below follow the supplied research discussion. Primary-reference links are provided for follow-up; full texts and numerical claims have not been independently verified in this environment. See [source and editorial notes](/guides/sources/).
:::

## Becker (1965): the opportunity cost of time

**Citation:** Gary S. Becker. “A Theory of the Allocation of Time.” *The Economic Journal* 75(299), 493–517.

**Paper:** [DOI / journal record](https://doi.org/10.2307/2228949).

- **Question and method:** A theoretical framework in which households combine time and purchased goods to produce valued activities and outcomes.
- **Key finding:** An activity's cost includes both money and forgone uses of time. Higher wages make non-work time more expensive but also increase income; the overall effect on leisure is ambiguous.
- **Scope and limitation:** A conceptual framework, not an estimate of historical leisure growth or a causal effect of wages.
- **Data:** No empirical dataset is required for the central theoretical contribution.

## Gronau (1977): separate home production from leisure

**Citation:** Reuben Gronau. “Leisure, Home Production, and Work—The Theory of the Allocation of Time Revisited.” *Journal of Political Economy* 85(6), 1099–1124.

**Paper:** [DOI / journal record](https://doi.org/10.1086/260629).

- **Question and method:** Extends time-allocation theory to distinguish market work, household production, and leisure.
- **Key finding:** Home production and leisure respond differently to wages and household circumstances. Fewer paid hours need not mean more leisure.
- **Scope and limitation:** A theoretical distinction; it does not supply a universal numerical relationship between work and leisure.
- **Data implication:** Diary activities must be classified separately rather than pooled as non-market time; see [time-use measurement](/data/leisure/).

## Aguiar and Hurst (2007): five decades of leisure trends

**Citation:** Mark Aguiar and Erik Hurst. “Measuring Trends in Leisure: The Allocation of Time over Five Decades.” *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 122(3), 969–1006.

**Paper:** [DOI / journal record](https://doi.org/10.1162/qjec.122.3.969).

- **Data and method:** Compares US time-use surveys from 1965 to 2003 using multiple definitions of leisure.
- **Key findings:** Weekly leisure increased by approximately **6–9 hours for men** and **4–8 hours for women**, depending on the definition. Men's gains largely reflected less paid work; women's gains largely reflected less household production. Gains increasingly favoured less-educated adults.
- **Interpretation:** Leisure inequality moved differently from wage inequality. More leisure does not itself establish higher welfare.
- **Limitations:** Survey harmonisation, sample restrictions, and activity definitions affect comparisons. These estimates are not directly comparable to whole-population century-long estimates.
- **Data guide:** [Historical US diaries and AHTUS](/data/leisure/#historical-us-diaries-ahtus).

## Ramey and Francis (2009): a century of work and leisure

**Citation:** Valerie A. Ramey and Neville Francis. “A Century of Work and Leisure.” *American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics* 1(2), 189–224.

**Paper:** [AEA article and associated materials](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mac.1.2.189).

- **Data and method:** Reconstructs twentieth-century US time allocation, accounting for schooling, home production, and population composition.
- **Key finding:** Per-capita leisure rose by only about **4–5 hours per week** over the century. Increased schooling offset much of young people's declining paid work; increasing female work hours offset declines among prime-age men.
- **Interpretation:** Falling market hours overstate leisure gains when other time commitments are omitted.
- **Limitations:** Historical reconstruction involves uncertain and uneven sources. Different periods, populations, and definitions explain why the headline result differs from Aguiar–Hurst.
- **Data guide:** [Historical reconstruction](/data/leisure/#historical-reconstruction).

## Ramey (2009): home production over the twentieth century

**Citation:** Valerie A. Ramey. “Time Spent in Home Production in the Twentieth-Century United States: New Estimates from Old Data.” *The Journal of Economic History* 69(1), 1–47.

**Paper:** [DOI / journal record](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022050709000333).

- **Data and method:** Combines historical time-use evidence with later individual diary data.
- **Key findings:** Between 1900 and 2005, weekly household-production time fell about **18 hours for prime-age women** and rose about **13 hours for prime-age men**. Across the whole population, including children and older people, home-production time per person increased slightly.
- **Interpretation:** Large declines in women's housework are not equivalent to a large aggregate decline in household work.
- **Limitations:** Age composition and historical source comparability matter; the sex-specific changes refer to prime-age adults, not everyone.
- **Data guide:** [Historical reconstruction](/data/leisure/#historical-reconstruction).

## Aguiar, Hurst, and Karabarbounis (2013): recession time use

**Citation:** Mark Aguiar, Erik Hurst, and Loukas Karabarbounis. “Time Use During the Great Recession.” *American Economic Review* 103(5), 1664–1696.

**Paper:** [AEA article](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.103.5.1664).

- **Data and method:** US time diaries and differences in recession severity across states are used to study the reallocation of lost paid-work hours.
- **Key findings:** The discussion's later summary assigns about **30%** of lost market-work time to home production and about **50%** to leisure under the paper's definition; sleep and television account for much of the latter.
- **Interpretation:** Households partly substitute their own time for purchased services. Lost work is not a one-for-one leisure gain.
- **Limitations:** Allocation shares depend on definitions and specification. The initial discussion used a 30–40% range for home production; treat these as approximate summaries pending table-level verification. The earlier working-paper title was “Time Use During Recessions.”
- **Data guide:** [ATUS](/data/leisure/#american-time-use-survey-atus).

## Krueger and Mueller (2012): unemployment and emotional well-being

**Citation:** Alan B. Krueger and Andreas I. Mueller. “Time Use, Emotional Well-Being, and Unemployment: Evidence from Longitudinal Data.” *American Economic Review* 102(3), 594–599.

**Paper:** [AEA article](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.102.3.594).

- **Data and method:** Longitudinal observations of unemployed people, including transitions back to employment, combining activity reports with emotional well-being.
- **Key findings:** Unemployed respondents reported greater sadness and less enjoyment of leisure-related activities. Re-employment was accompanied by sharply lower household-production time and reduced sadness.
- **Interpretation:** Voluntary time off and involuntary non-employment need not have equal value. The study does **not** show that leisure itself causes unhappiness.
- **Limitations:** Employment transitions and emotional changes need careful causal interpretation. The longitudinal study is not interchangeable with the standard ATUS repeated cross-section.
- **Data access:** Consult the article's underlying study description and access conditions; no public panel download is asserted here.

## Aguiar, Bils, Charles, and Hurst (2021): leisure technology

**Citation:** Mark Aguiar, Mark Bils, Kerwin Kofi Charles, and Erik Hurst. “Leisure Luxuries and the Labor Supply of Young Men.” *Journal of Political Economy* 129(2), 337–382.

**Paper:** [NBER working-paper version](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23552). Distinguish this version from the 2021 publication.

- **Data and method:** Time-use evidence on young men aged 21–30, combined with a model of leisure demand and labour supply.
- **Key findings:** Gaming and recreational computing behave as “leisure luxuries”: their use rises disproportionately with total leisure. The model attributes an approximately **2% reduction in young men's work hours** to improved leisure technology, explaining a substantial part of their decline relative to older men.
- **Interpretation:** This is model-based attribution, not experimental evidence that gaming caused lower employment.
- **Limitations:** Counterfactual results depend on the model and estimated preferences; the result is not an estimate for all workers.
- **Data guide:** [US time-use data](/data/leisure/); consult the paper for its exact additional sources and sample construction.

## Further reading: a synthesis

Mark Aguiar and Erik Hurst, **“The Macroeconomics of Time Allocation.”** [NBER review working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17995). A broader synthesis covering leisure, childcare, and home production over the life cycle and business cycle; not an additional independent empirical estimate.
