---
title: Glossary of key terms
description: Plain-language definitions of welfare, productivity, GDP, leisure, and research methods used in this wiki.
---

Use this glossary to interpret the paper and findings summaries. Definitions describe how terms are used here; a paper's own measurement choices take precedence.

## Autarky

An economy with no international trade. Historical embargoes may approximate autarky without achieving it. This differs from capital controls and from the closed-economy assumption in a model. See [trade research](/papers/trade/).

## Causal effect

The change in an outcome caused by a change in an input or policy, relative to what would otherwise have happened. A correlation alone does not establish causation: other factors or reverse causation may explain it. See [trade identification examples](/papers/trade/).

## Consumption-equivalent welfare

A way to express a welfare difference as an equivalent change in consumption under a specified model. It can value leisure, longevity, and inequality alongside consumption. It is not an observed change in GDP. See [Jones–Klenow](/papers/leisure-productivity/#jones-and-klenow-2016-valuing-leisure-beyond-gdp).

## Counterfactual

An alternative outcome that was not observed, such as a country's income if it had remained open to trade. Studies estimate it using comparison groups, statistical designs, or models. Conclusions depend on whether that alternative is credible. See [observed outcomes versus counterfactuals](/findings/trade/).

## Elasticity

The percentage change in one variable associated with, or caused by, a 1% change in another, depending on the research design. An income–trade elasticity is not an annual GDP growth rate. See [Feyrer](/papers/trade/#feyrer-2021-the-suez-canal-natural-experiment).

## GDP: gross domestic product

The value of final goods and services produced within an economy over a period. GDP measures production, not overall well-being. Most unpaid household work and the direct enjoyment of leisure are outside its production boundary. See [GDP and leisure](/papers/leisure-productivity/).

## GDP per capita

GDP divided by the resident population; also called GDP per person. It is an average, not the income of a typical household or a measure of how income is distributed. For comparisons, distinguish real growth over time from PPP-adjusted levels across countries. See [GDP data](/data/trade/#world-development-indicators-wdi).

## GDP growth

The rate at which GDP changes over time. A one-time increase in the level of GDP need not imply permanently faster growth. Total GDP growth and GDP-per-capita growth can differ because population changes. See [trade findings](/findings/trade/).

## GNP: gross national product

A measure of production-related income accruing to a country's residents, including net primary income from abroad. Unlike GDP, it follows residents rather than only domestic production. Modern accounts generally use the term gross national income (GNI). A welfare loss expressed as a share of GNP is not necessarily a fall in measured GNP; see [Irwin's embargo study](/papers/trade/#irwin-2005-the-jeffersonian-embargo).

## Home production

Unpaid activities that produce household goods or services, such as cooking, cleaning, and care. Less paid work may mean more home production rather than more leisure. Surveys differ in their treatment of care and simultaneous activities. See [Gronau](/papers/leisure/#gronau-1977-separate-home-production-from-leisure).

## Income effect and substitution effect

Two channels through which a wage change can affect time allocation. Higher income may allow someone to afford more leisure (**income effect**). A higher wage also raises the earnings forgone by taking time off, encouraging work instead (**substitution effect**). Their net effect is not fixed. See [Becker](/papers/leisure/#becker-1965-the-opportunity-cost-of-time).

## Instrumental variable

A variable used to isolate variation in an input that is not driven by the same confounders as the outcome. It must affect the input and satisfy identification assumptions, including affecting the outcome only through the relevant channel. These assumptions need argument and evidence. See [sunset timing and sleep](/papers/leisure-productivity/#gibson-and-shrader-2018-sleep-and-labour-productivity).

## Labour productivity

Output per unit of labour input, often real GDP per hour worked. It can rise because of skills, capital, technology, organisation, or changes in which workers and sectors are measured. It does not measure effort alone. Higher output per hour does not guarantee higher total output. See [the hours–output example](/findings/leisure-productivity/#output-per-hour-and-total-output-can-move-differently).

## Labour supply

The amount of paid work people are willing and able to provide. It includes decisions about whether to work and how many hours to work. Observed employment also depends on employers' demand and constraints; it is not a pure measure of preferences. See [taxes and market hours](/papers/leisure-productivity/#prescott-2004-taxes-and-market-working-hours).

## Leisure

Time outside activities classified as work or other obligations under a study's definition. Narrow measures may focus on recreation; broader ones may include sleep or personal care. Time outside employment is not automatically leisure. Always check the activity categories and population. See [leisure measurement](/data/leisure/#choose-and-define-the-measure-first).

## Nominal and real measures

**Nominal** values use current prices. **Real** measures adjust for price changes to distinguish changes in quantities from inflation. Cross-country level comparisons also require a common valuation basis, often PPP; inflation adjustment alone is not enough. See [output and hours data](/data/leisure-productivity/#national-output-hours-and-employment).

## Opportunity cost

The value of the best alternative forgone when making a choice. An hour of leisure may cost forgone earnings, but the relevant alternative may instead be study, care, or another activity. See [Becker's allocation-of-time framework](/papers/leisure/#becker-1965-the-opportunity-cost-of-time).

## Percentage points

The arithmetic difference between two percentages. A growth rate rising from 2% to 3% increases by **1 percentage point**, or by **50% relative to its initial rate**. These are different statements. The [Wacziarg–Welch summary](/papers/trade/#wacziarg-and-welch-2008-liberalisation-and-growth) reports percentage points.

## Productivity

Output relative to the inputs used to produce it. Always specify the input and output: labour productivity, total factor productivity, wages, and total GDP are different measures. This wiki uses **labour productivity** when discussing GDP per hour. See [productivity research](/papers/leisure-productivity/).

## Purchasing power parity (PPP)

A conversion that accounts for differences in price levels across economies. PPP-adjusted measures help compare the goods and services that incomes can buy, rather than converting only at market exchange rates. Results depend on the benchmark and data vintage. See [PWT and GDP sources](/data/trade/).

## Repeated cross-section and panel

A **repeated cross-section** samples people at successive dates, usually different people each time. A **panel** follows the same units over time. Country trends from repeated cross-sections are not evidence of each person's trajectory. See [ATUS](/data/leisure/#american-time-use-survey-atus).

## Tax wedge

The gap between an employer's labour cost and a worker's net reward, created by taxes and contributions; precise definitions vary. Consumption taxes may also affect the incentive to exchange work for consumption in macroeconomic models. Check the paper's measure before comparing estimates. See [Prescott](/papers/leisure-productivity/#prescott-2004-taxes-and-market-working-hours).

## Total factor productivity (TFP)

Output relative to a combined measure of inputs, typically capital and labour. In growth accounting it is often calculated as a residual, so it can reflect measurement error and omitted influences as well as technology or efficiency. It is not interchangeable with GDP per hour. See [Penn World Table](/data/trade/#penn-world-table-pwt).

## Trade openness and trade liberalisation

**Trade openness** may refer to observed trade exposure or to policy; specify which. **Trade liberalisation** means reducing trade barriers. A high trade/GDP ratio does not by itself prove low barriers, and a low ratio does not establish autarky. See [trade policy data](/data/trade/#trade-policy-wits-and-unctad-trains).

## Welfare

Economic well-being as defined by a study's framework. It may account for consumption, leisure, health, longevity, risk, and distribution. It does **not** mean government benefit payments in these research summaries. Welfare is broader than GDP and is not directly observable as one universally agreed statistic. See [welfare beyond GDP](/papers/leisure-productivity/#jones-and-klenow-2016-valuing-leisure-beyond-gdp).

## Reading definitions alongside evidence

These are explanatory definitions, not new empirical findings. Follow the linked paper entries and data guides for context, primary-reference links, and verification limits. The [source notes](/guides/sources/) explain what has and has not been checked.
